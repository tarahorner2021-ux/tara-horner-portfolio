Overview

Luke Pope Leather is a one-person leather shop — wallets, belts, and holsters built to order on a single bench. Before this project, there was no shop front at all: orders came in over text and Instagram DMs, in-progress pieces lived wherever Luke last set them down, and there was no way for a customer to see what was possible before asking.

I designed a mobile shop for a business that will likely never have more than one person running it: browsing what's ready now, requesting a fully custom piece, seeing the shop's actual body of work, and tracking an order after it's placed.

The constraint shaped everything: every screen had to work for a single maker checking his phone between projects, not a team running a storefront.

The challenge

A one-person shop, and a phone-sized storefront

Luke doesn't have a backend team, a warehouse, or a customer service line — he has a workbench and a phone. Whatever this became had to be maintainable by one person, in whatever time is left over after the leather work itself.

Made-to-order businesses like this one have a specific problem: most of what's for sale doesn't exist yet. A standard e-commerce grid, everything in stock, everything buy-now, doesn't tell the truth about how the shop actually works.

And the trust problem is real. A customer paying for a custom holster or wallet sight unseen needs a reason to believe the maker can deliver, before they've ever held the leather.

The opportunity

Sell the process, not just the product

The insight: for a made-to-order shop, the body of work is the sales pitch. A gallery of finished pieces does more to build confidence than any product description could.

The opportunity was to design around the two states real inventory actually has — ready to ship today, or coming soon on a real date — instead of forcing every piece into a single always-in-stock model.

And for anything that isn't a standard piece, the shop needed its own front door: a custom order request built for a maker who works from a description and a reference photo, not a configurator.

Design approach

Four screens, one bench

Shop

The home screen is the shop, split by a single toggle: Ready to ship and Coming soon.

  • Ready to ship is real inventory, in stock today
  • Coming soon carries the date each piece will be ready, so a customer can decide whether to wait

Custom order

For anything that isn't already in the shop, a direct request form stands in for a configurator most custom pieces wouldn't fit anyway.

  • Name, contact, an optional need-by date, a plain-language description, and an optional reference file
  • Luke reads every request himself before it becomes an order

Past work

The gallery does double duty as portfolio and proof of process — the only place a customer can see the work before they commit to it.

  • Category filters split finished pieces — wallets, belts, holsters, custom
  • A dedicated Process filter shows the bench itself: tools laid out, a piece mid-build

Key UX principles

Solutions

A storefront that tells the truth about how the shop works

Process as proof

A gallery of finished pieces sells the product. A gallery that also shows tool layouts and pieces mid-build sells the maker, which matters more when a customer is paying for something that doesn't exist yet.

Two shop states

Ready to ship and Coming soon live on the same screen, so nothing is sold as in-stock when it isn't.

Reference-first custom form

Free-text description plus an optional file upload, matched to how Luke actually works from a client's reference.

Ship-date transparency

Every coming-soon piece carries the date it'll be ready, not a vague "pre-order."

Plain order tracking

Delivered, shipped, or in progress — laid out the same way a text-message update would read.

One-handed everything

Add to bag, send a request, check an order — every primary action sits within a thumb's reach of the bottom nav.

Outcome

The shop went from word-of-mouth and DMs to something a customer can actually browse, request, and track.

Instead of...

Orders over text and DMs → no visual record → a customer buying on trust alone

Intended experience

Browse real inventory or request custom → see the actual body of work → track the order after it ships

Luke Pope Leather stayed a one-person shop the whole way through — this is the version of e-commerce sized to fit on a workbench, not the other way around.

Project impact

A storefront sized for one pair of hands

The biggest shift wasn't visual, it was structural: designing every flow around the assumption that the person running the business and the person building the product are the same person, with the same twenty free minutes a day.

The design decisions that mattered most:

  • Splitting inventory into Ready to ship and Coming soon instead of forcing a single in-stock model
  • A custom order form built around a description and a reference file, not a rigid configurator
  • The Past work gallery, with a Process filter that shows the making, not just the finished piece
  • Order tracking simple enough to replace a string of text-message updates
